MIDA's board: seven seats, three tied to project geography

The Military Installation Development Authority exercises municipal-style land-use and public-finance powers within eight active project areas spread across multiple Utah counties — with a seven-member board no one directly elects. Three of the governor's five seats carry an automatic vacancy rule tied to local office; the board's two legislative-leadership seats don't. Here's the current roster, the appointing authority behind each seat, and the one seat with no automatic-vacancy provision at all.

Governance Appointments Vacancy Gap
01 — New to this?

Three seats, an automatic-vacancy rule, and the seats without one

In plain language

MIDA's board has seven voting members. Under Utah Code § 63H-1-302, the governor appoints five, the president of the Senate appoints one, and the speaker of the House appoints one. An eighth, nonvoting seat can be added by board resolution.

Three of the governor's five appointments must be local elected officials — mayors or members of a local legislative body — near an active MIDA project area. When MIDA has more than three active project areas in different jurisdictions, the governor must appoint at least one local official near the highest-value project, one near the second-highest, and a third eligible local official who may come from elsewhere. The governor still picks the individual. What's automatic is the vacancy: if one of these three appointees stops holding the qualifying local office, the MIDA seat opens up by statute, and the governor appoints a new eligible local official.

The board's other two seats — the Senate President's and House Speaker's appointees — don't carry that same automatic-vacancy trigger. That difference is the structural story here.

03 — The vacancy rule, documented

What actually triggers a seat change

The clearest example of the automatic-vacancy rule in action sits in Wasatch County, tied to MIDA's Military Recreation Facility project area — the Jordanelle/Deer Valley expansion.

Jan. 7, 2025

  • Steve Farrell, a retiring Wasatch County Council member, formally honored via board proclamation for his contributions to "the Military Recreation Facility Project Area" and its public infrastructure districts
  • Karl McMillan, also a Wasatch County Council member, welcomed as a new board member at the same meeting — Gov. Spencer Cox had appointed him the day before, Jan. 6, 2025

April – May 2026

  • McMillan resigned from the Wasatch County Council and his MIDA-related positions in April 2026 — this is what triggered the statutory MIDA vacancy, not any change to the project area itself
  • Erik Rowland, Wasatch County Council Seat C, appointed to fill the vacancy in May 2026

What this sequence demonstrates is the automatic-vacancy rule tied to holding local office — not that a particular MIDA seat permanently belongs to "whoever holds" a specific position. When a gubernatorial appointee occupying one of the three local seats leaves the qualifying office, the MIDA seat becomes vacant by statute, and the governor appoints another eligible local official.

Gage Froerer appears to occupy another of the three local-official seats. He has served on MIDA since 2019 while serving as a Weber County commissioner, although the appointment record identifying his precise statutory seat was not located.

04 — The seats without an automatic-vacancy rule

What happens when the chairman loses his election

Not every seat on the board carries that automatic trigger — and the difference is where the real accountability gap sits.

Sen. Stuart Adams has served on MIDA's board since 2008 and currently chairs it. That appointment came after his time in the Utah House (2002-2006) had ended, and before he joined the Senate in 2009 — his MIDA seat actually predates his Senate service. His original appointment came from then-Gov. Jon Huntsman Jr. Today, Utah's boards and commissions records list him as "self-appointed" to the Senate President's seat — a function of his holding that office now, not a fixed personal appointment.

Adams lost his Republican primary in June 2026. His Senate District 7 seat ends at the close of the year. Senate Republicans won't select a new Senate President until a closed-door caucus vote after the November 3, 2026 general election.

The gap, plainly

Board members serve four-year terms and continue serving until a successor is "duly appointed and qualified." They also serve at the pleasure of their appointing authority and can be replaced at any time.

The three local-official seats carry an automatic vacancy when the appointee leaves the qualifying local office.
The Senate President's and House Speaker's seats carry no equivalent automatic-vacancy trigger.
Adams's departure from the Senate does not, by itself, remove him from MIDA's board.
He could remain until the incoming Senate President either removes him or appoints and qualifies a successor.

In practical terms: the mechanism that automatically opens a Wasatch County Council seat when its occupant leaves local office doesn't apply to the seat currently held by the board's own chairman.

On the nonvoting seat: MIDA's resolution appointing Jeff Moss closely associates the position with the GOEO Executive Director's office — it frames the value of having GOEO's director specifically on the board — although it formally appoints Moss by name, for the remainder of the GOEO director's term (expiring March 2027) and a subsequent four-year term that automatically renews until another appointment is made.

05 — The pattern

An automatic-vacancy rule that applies to some seats and not others

Utah law treats MIDA's three local seats differently from its two legislative-leadership appointments. A local-seat appointee automatically loses the MIDA position upon leaving the qualifying mayoral or local-legislative office — Wasatch County's seat has changed hands twice under that rule since 2025. The statute imposes no comparable automatic vacancy when the Senate President's or House Speaker's appointee leaves elected office.

Instead, those board members serve four-year terms, remain until a successor is appointed and qualified, and may be replaced at any time by their appointing authority. Adams's departure from the Senate would not by itself remove him from MIDA — he could remain until the appropriate appointing authority acts.

Does this mean Adams is doing something improper by staying on the board?

No — nothing here alleges wrongdoing. The point is structural: Utah law does not apply the same automatic-vacancy rule to this category of appointment that it applies to the three local-official seats. Whether Adams stays or is replaced is entirely up to the next Senate President.

Is Stevenson's appointing authority confirmed?

Not directly. By elimination — the board's other five seats are independently identified as governor appointees (Shepherd, Rowland, Froerer, Harter, Ostermiller), and Adams is confirmed as the Senate President's seat — Stevenson's seat is the only one that can logically be the House Speaker's. But that's inference, not a record. Utah's own boards-and-commissions site (boards.utah.gov) blocks automated access, so this wasn't confirmed by pulling the record directly, and no source found states outright that Stevenson was appointed by the Speaker. Treat this line as unconfirmed pending a direct records check.
