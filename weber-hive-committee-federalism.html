FED

Federalism Commission

Senate interim · House interim · joint body · established 2013, rebuilt 2025
Studies and recommends legislation on federal–state relations — but the statute doesn’t stop there: it tasks a public university with designing a national organization and recruiting private-sector help to sell it to other states.

A body established in 2013 was rebuilt across three 2025 bills into something with real machinery: a university-housed institute tasked by statute with building a 50-state network, a PR contract that helped fund an actual lawsuit, and a pipeline that routes every federal guidance letter received by any state agency to its chairs. Its public record shows no engagement when the target moved from federal land to federal AI policy.

Movement Two

The Machinery Built Since 2025

Three bills in one year turned a study body into something with statutory teeth and a funding line.

HB 379 (2025)

Expanded the Commission’s reach

Let the Commission add nonvoting members and provide “assistance” to interim committees on federalism-related bills — the procedural hook that lets it help push sovereignty legislation through committees it doesn’t sit on.

HB 488 (2025) — Utah Code § 53H-4-703 / -704

The one that matters most

Statutorily tasks Utah Valley University’s Center for Constitutional Studies and the Herbert Institute for Public Policy to: train government employees in sovereignty doctrine; convene an annual multi-state conference; study and design a state-led national federalism organization; and coordinate with private-sector actors to conduct out-of-state outreach building political support in other states. This is not aspirational language — it is the verbatim statute.

SB 198 (2025) — Federal Guidance Letter Amendments

A live surveillance pipeline

Requires every state agency to forward any federal guidance letter it receives to the Commission chairs and Legislative Management Committee within 15 days. The Commission built a review protocol with the Sutherland Institute and maintains a tracking spreadsheet flagging letters that “appear to violate the principles of federalism.”

SB 100 (2026) — Federalism Revisions

Chief Sponsor Sen. Keven J. Stratton, House Sponsor Rep. Ken Ivory

Extends the SB 198 federal-guidance-letter reporting requirement to certain education entities; requires agencies to submit an electronic report to the Commission recommending how to handle each letter; requires the Commission to report annually to the Legislative Management Committee; and provides for “the registration and use of certain federalism related trademarks.”

“Coordinate with private sector actors who agree to conduct out-of-state outreach to build national support for the commission’s federalism efforts.” — Utah Code § 53H-4-703(4), verbatim
Movement Three

Where It Used Its Authority — And Where It Didn’t

The pattern holds every time it’s checked: real, funded, staffed machinery whenever the target is outward — federal jurisdiction, other states’ legislatures. Silence, with no recorded explanation, on the two moments that would have added accountability to the Commission’s own use of power.

Used it

  • HB3002 (June 2024 special session) put $2.14M into a “Federal Overreach Restricted Account,” which became a $2.6M PR contract (Penna Powers) that helped fund Utah’s actual lawsuit seeking ~18.5M acres of BLM land.
  • SB 158 (2025) forced PLPCO to produce a full audit of its own federal land applications, 2014–2024.
  • Commissioned a reanalysis of the 1957 Eisenhower jurisdiction study, finding 96%+ of federally controlled Utah land carries no asserted federal governing claim — built explicitly to support 2026 legislation.
  • SB 198’s 15-day letter pipeline, described above, is live and staffed.

Didn’t use it

  • Sen. Nate Blouin’s proposed federal-funds accountability amendment to SB 57 was never brought to a formal vote. No committee record on file explains why.
  • The Commission’s own requested bill for the 2027 session, “Public Lands Planning Amendments,” shows: Requested: Yes · Recommended: No. They asked for their own bill, then declined to send it forward.
  • After a November 2025 briefing finding federal funds make up ~30% of Utah’s budget — its single largest funding source — the Commission’s output was a vote to “open a bill file” for a federal-funds risk dashboard. No dashboard has been built or funded since.
Movement Four

When the Pressure Ran the Other Way

HB 286, the AI Transparency Act, is the inverse of every case above: federal deregulatory pressure killing a state regulation. It is also the one case where the Commission’s public record shows no engagement at all.

Jan 27, 2026
Committee passes HB 286 8–0–2: tiered civil penalties, whistleblower protections, child-safety chatbot rules, frontier-model risk disclosure.
Feb 5, 2026
Circled on the House floor by voice vote — pulled from an immediate vote, no explanation on the record.
Feb 12, 2026
White House Office of Intergovernmental Affairs sends a one-sentence letter to Sen. Kirk Cullimore. David Sacks named by multiple outlets as driving the opposition. White House officials also spoke with sponsor Doug Fiefia directly, telling him no changes would satisfy them.
Feb–Mar 2026
26-day stall. No floor action.
Mar 3, 2026
Sent from the 3rd Reading Calendar back to House Rules — three days before session end.
Mar 6, 2026, 11:59 PM
Enacting clause struck. Filed among bills not passed — the identical kill mechanism documented for HB 468 one session earlier.
Mar 20, 2026
Six weeks later, the White House releases its own “National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence,” asking Congress for child-safety AI rules with no timeline attached.
The administration killed a state bill that already had child-safety protections ready to sign, then six weeks later asked Congress for a federal version with no timeline attached.
Why didn’t the Federalism Commission get involved?

No discussion of HB 286 appears anywhere in the Commission’s publicly available agendas or minutes. Utah’s own institutional posture was conciliatory, not defensive: Speaker Mike Schultz said lawmakers would “continue studying the legislation” and that he understood the administration’s concerns. Senate leadership had told reporters in January — before the letter even arrived — that they believed Utah had already won over the Trump administration through its 2023 AI regulatory sandbox approach.

One possible reading: the Commission’s leadership and programming — Ivory, Stratton, PLPCO, the ALEC/SPN-adjacent events — are built around fighting federal regulatory overreach on land, guns, and funding strings. HB 286 was the opposite case, and defending it would have meant invoking federalism against the same White House the Commission’s own coalition is broadly aligned with on deregulation. Not a missed opportunity so much as a mismatch of ideology.

In Their Own Words

What the Commission’s own members and allies have said on the record about why this exists.

Rep. Ken Ivory, House Chair

KSL.com, March 10, 2025

“The national government, the federal government, is a government of limited jurisdiction. It can only do the things that were specifically delegated to it. The state government is a government of general jurisdiction.”

Rep. Ken Ivory, House Chair

Deseret News, March 8, 2025

“It’s a big deal because only by maintaining the balance in that state-federal relationship do people have their greatest governing voice… their governing voice is at the state level.”

Senate President Stuart Adams (not a Commission member)

KSL.com, March 10, 2025 — asked why federalism was such a big focus of the session

“Probably because we love the state of Utah, and we think we do things a lot better than our federal government does.”

Former Gov. Gary Herbert (Herbert Institute namesake)

Deseret News, Feb. 24, 2025, on HB 488

“We need to have a return to the federalism. It means a relationship between the states and the federal government as equal partners, and things have evolved to the point where if we’re even thought about it all as a partner it’s as a junior partner — and that’s not the way the Founding Fathers envisioned it.”

Sen. Keven J. Stratton, Senate Chair

Declare, Jan. 14, 2026

“Who governs, if anyone?” — the framing question Stratton uses to introduce Utah’s approach in outside presentations, per the outlet’s direct quote.
