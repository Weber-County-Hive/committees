MIDA's board: seven seats, three that move with the project

The Military Installation Development Authority zones land, issues bonds, and levies taxes across project areas from Clearfield to Box Elder County — with a seven-member board no one elects. Utah statute ties three of those seats to whichever local officials happen to sit near an active project area. Here's the current roster, the appointing authority behind each seat, and the one seat with no rotation mechanism at all.

01 — New to this?

A board built to move with the map

In plain language

MIDA's board has seven voting members. Under Utah Code § 63H-1-302, the governor appoints five, the president of the Senate appoints one, and the speaker of the House appoints one. An eighth, nonvoting seat can be added by the board itself.

Three of the governor's five appointments aren't fixed offices — they belong to whoever holds local elected office "adjacent or in close proximity" to an active MIDA project area. As MIDA opens new project areas in new counties, or a local official leaves office, that seat is designed to change hands. It isn't a loophole; it's how the statute is written. What it means in practice is that MIDA's board composition shifts with where MIDA is currently developing land — not with any electoral check from the people living there.

03 — The rotating seat, documented

Three people, one seat, under two years

The clearest example of the project-dependent design sits in Wasatch County, tied to MIDA's Military Recreation Facility project area — the Jordanelle/Deer Valley expansion.

Jan. 7, 2025

  • Steve Farrell, Wasatch County Council Member, formally thanked and retired via board resolution tied specifically to "the Military Recreation Facility Project Area"
  • Karl McMillan, also a Wasatch County Council Member, welcomed as his replacement at the same meeting

May 2026

  • Erik Rowland, Wasatch County Council Seat C, appointed to the board
  • Replaces McMillan — the same seat, its third occupant in under two years

None of these are removals for cause. This is the statute working as written: the seat belongs to whoever holds a particular local office while that project area is active, and it changes hands as the officeholder changes.

Gage Froerer's seat works the same way — appointed in 2019 as the Weber County Commission's representative. Froerer is separately documented in our reporting on the Nordic Village PID for a sworn Weber Basin Water Conservancy District conflict-of-interest disclosure, signed January 15, 2026, that listed only two board memberships — and did not list MIDA.

04 — The seat with no rotation mechanism

What happens when the chairman loses his election

Not every seat on the board works this way — and the exception is where the accountability gap actually sits.

Sen. Stuart Adams has chaired MIDA's board since 2008, two years before he was elected to the Utah House. His original appointment came from then-Gov. Jon Huntsman Jr. Today, Utah's own boards and commissions records list him as "self-appointed" to the Senate President's seat — a function of his holding that office, not a fixed personal appointment.

Adams lost his Republican primary in June 2026. His Senate District 7 seat ends at the close of the year. Senate Republicans won't select a new Senate President until a closed-door caucus vote after the November 3, 2026 general election.

The gap, plainly

Utah law gives the incoming Senate President the authority to name Adams's replacement on MIDA's board. Nothing in statute requires it.

Board members serve at the pleasure of their appointing authority and can be removed at any time.
Service does NOT automatically end when an appointee leaves elected office — except for the mayor/local-legislative-body seats, which are explicitly tied to holding that local office.
The Senate President's seat is not in that category.
Unless the next Senate President affirmatively acts, Adams could keep voting on MIDA's project areas after leaving elected office entirely.

In practical terms: the mechanism that swaps a Wasatch County Council member as project areas shift doesn't apply to the seat currently held by the board's own chairman. That seat could sit with someone no longer holding any elected office at all — for however long it takes a new Senate President to decide otherwise.

05 — The pattern

Accountability that moves — except where it matters most

Three of MIDA's seats are designed to rotate with the map: as project areas open and close, the local officials sitting on the board change with them. That's a real, working mechanism — Wasatch County's seat alone has had three occupants in under two years.

But the board's two legislative seats — including the chairmanship, held continuously since 2008 — carry no equivalent mechanism. They rotate only when the appointing authority chooses to act. For one of those seats, the appointing authority itself won't exist in its current form until sometime after November 3, 2026.

Does this mean Adams is doing something improper by staying on the board?

No — nothing here alleges wrongdoing. The point is structural: Utah law simply does not require a board seat to change hands when its holder leaves the elected office that seat is nominally tied to, for this particular category of appointment. Whether Adams stays or is replaced is entirely up to the next Senate President.

Is Stevenson's appointing authority confirmed?

Not directly. By elimination — the board's other five seats are independently identified as governor appointees (Shepherd, Rowland, Froerer, Harter, Ostermiller), and Adams is confirmed as the Senate President's seat — Stevenson's seat is the only one that can logically be the House Speaker's. But that's inference, not a record. Utah's own boards-and-commissions site (boards.utah.gov) blocks automated access, so this wasn't confirmed by pulling the record directly, and no source found states outright that Stevenson was appointed by the Speaker. Treat this line as unconfirmed pending a direct records check.

Where does this come from?
